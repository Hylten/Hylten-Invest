The Christian steward does not hold assets indefinitely. Scripture prescribes a horizon to every obligation. Leviticus 25 establishes the year of Jubilee: every fifty years, property returns to its original family, debts are forgiven, and the economic order resets. The principle is not accumulation without limit. It is stewardship with a boundary. The modern family office that holds illiquid private assets for a decade without a liquidity plan treats that boundary as optional.

The institutional market now offers a channel that mirrors that principle. Per the CAIA LP GUIDE 2026, secondary volume reached 121 billion USD in the first half of 2026, and GP-led transactions command 54 percent of that flow. At current rates, that is over one trillion SEK in scheduled release. The secondary market is not a departure from the Jubilee pattern. It is an institutional echo of the same truth: no obligation outlives its purpose, and no family loses its inheritance forever.

## The Jubilee Pattern in Institutional Form

The Jubilee was not a market exit. It was a scheduled reset that preserved the underlying ownership structure while releasing the encumbrance. The modern steward who approaches the secondary market with the same logic does not abandon the asset class. The steward rebalances the portfolio around mission-aligned exposures while preserving the capital base.

The parallel is exact. Jubilee releases the land to its original family. The secondary release returns the capital to the steward's control. Jubilee forgives the debt. The secondary price, when entered with discipline, forgives the liquidity premium that would otherwise erode the intergenerational transfer.

The stewardship question is not whether liquidity exists. The question is whether the steward uses it faithfully. A covenant-based approach treats the secondary transaction as part of the capital structure, not as an exit from it. The steward sells with intention, redeploys with purpose, and preserves the generational mission intact.

## Covenant Discipline in the Purchase

The stewardship standard applies most sharply on the buy side. The family office that purchases secondary interests must price the asset at its true performance, not at the seller's convenience. The GP-led continuation fund that offers a premium to NAV invites the steward to verify that premium against independent observation.

Per the AngelInvestorNetwork GP-Led Secondaries Guide, GP-led transactions in H1 2026 priced at a premium of two to four percent to NAV, while LP-to-LP transactions priced at a discount of four to seven percent. The three percentage points of spread is the GP's information rent, and the steward who accepts it without verification pays the convenience cost that Jubilee was designed to prevent.

The discipline has three dimensions. First, the steward evaluates whether the secondary price reflects the asset's true earning power or merely the seller's liquidity constraint. A seller that needs to exit for cash flow reasons will accept a price below NAV. A seller that wants to rotate into a different mandate will accept a price at parity. The steward's first duty is to distinguish the two.

Second, the steward structures the purchase to preserve the existing covenant protections. The continuation vehicle that rolls the portfolio into a new vehicle must maintain the same reporting regime, the same collateral framework, and the same amortization profile. The steward who accepts a structure without these protections exchanges certainty for convenience.

Third, the steward redeploys the capital in alignment with the generational mission. The Jubilee returned the land to the family. The steward's capital redeployment must return the portfolio to its mission-aligned core, not to the highest bidder for immediate yield.

## The Generational Transfer Window

The wealth transfer now underway makes the secondary question urgent. Per Fortune and Bank of America Private Bank research, up to 124 trillion dollars will transfer from Baby Boomers to younger generations, roughly 1.3 million miljarder SEK at current exchange rates. Of that, only 23 percent sits in family businesses that will transfer intact, per the same research.

The Christian steward faces a particular challenge in this window. Per National Christian Foundation research, only 28 percent of Christian Millennials who will inherit wealth say their family has a transfer plan. The remaining 72 percent face a stewardship crisis at the moment of greatest responsibility. The secondary market is not the solution to that crisis. The covenant structure around the secondary is.

The steward who uses secondary liquidity to restructure around mission-aligned assets preserves the transfer. The steward who uses it to abandon the mission abandons the inheritance. The difference is visible in the price discipline: the faithful steward buys when the market dislocates value, and sells when the market prices it correctly.

## The Three Covenant Questions

Every secondary transaction passes through three covenant questions. The first is whether the steward is selling from strength or from need. Strength sells at premiums that reflect accurate pricing. Need sells at discounts that reflect urgency. The steward who can identify the difference buys the need-driven seller's position and adds the premium to the portfolio's yield.

The second question is whether the purchase price preserves the asset's operational integrity. Private assets derive value from their operational capacity, not their liquidity. The steward who purchases a secondary interest must retain the operational rights that make the asset valuable. A secondary fund that strips reporting and oversight from the underlying assets destroys the very value the steward seeks to preserve.

The third question is whether the deployment aligns with the generational mission. The Jubilee returned the land to the family that originally cultivated it. The steward's redeployment must return the capital to the investments that serve the family's stated purpose. Capital deployed without mission alignment becomes the very drift that stewardship was meant to prevent.

## The Dry Powder Imperative

Per the AkinGump 2026 Perspectives in Private Equity report, industry dry powder reached 315 billion USD by Q3 2025, a historic high that fuels buy-side capacity for secondary purchases. At current exchange rates, that represents over 3.4 trillion SEK in uninvested capital waiting for deployment. The capitalization of that dry powder creates a buyer's market dynamic in the secondary channel.

The steward who enters this market with covenant discipline captures the pricing differential that convenience buyers leave on the table. The GP-led premium compresses when multiple qualified purchasers compete, and the faithful steward bids not with the highest price but with the most precise valuation. The information edge that the GP holds erodes when multiple stewards verify independently.

The steward's advantage is not capital size. The steward's advantage is the ability to hold through the Jubilee cycle without pressure to exit. Private equity funds that need to return capital before the fifth year of the fund's life sell at deeper discounts. The steward who can hold for the full cycle purchases those positions at prices that compound the portfolio's long-term return.

## The Stewardship Standard

The standard is not avoidance. Scripture does not forbid borrowing or secondary sales. Proverbs 22:7 describes a reality rather than a prohibition. The standard is structure: finite terms, real security, honest reporting, and a scheduled release.

The steward evaluates the price, structures the covenant, and redeploys with purpose. The convenience buyer accepts the GP's premium and leaves the discipline to others. The family office that builds secondary discipline into its allocation framework builds on the Jubilee.

The inheritance in view is not only the asset. It is the character that manages the asset through the Jubilee window, into the next generation, with capital intact and purpose unshaken. The steward who enters the secondary market with covenant discipline does not trade the asset. The steward trades the structure, and the structure determines whether the inheritance survives.
