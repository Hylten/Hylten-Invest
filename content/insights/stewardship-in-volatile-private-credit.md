## The current private credit landscape continues to be defined by a structural gap rather than a cyclical aberration

The spread dislocation observed across North American and European middle-market borrowers is an output of regulatory compression and balance sheet retrenchment among traditional lenders. The vacuum is not a symptom of weakened asset bases but of capital architecture that has not kept pace with real-economy demand. This gap has created a market where the durability of capital stewardship has become a differentiator more powerful than scale, branding, or origination velocity.

The structural nature of this dislocation is evident in the persistence of elevated spreads despite the normalization of base rates. In the North American middle market, direct lending spreads have remained 200 to 300 basis points above pre-2020 levels, even as public credit markets have tightened. This is not a temporary mispricing but a reflection of the new equilibrium between risk and return in a post-Basel III world. European markets exhibit similar dynamics, with German Mittelstand borrowers facing spreads 150 to 250 basis points wider than historical averages, despite stable operational performance.

This gap is not uniform. It is most pronounced in sectors where regulatory capital weights are highest - energy infrastructure, industrial equipment leasing, and specialized logistics. These are not speculative or cyclical industries but the backbone of real economic activity. The dislocation is a function of capital architecture, not asset quality. This distinction is critical for allocators seeking to deploy capital with precision.

## The regime shift

The private credit market entered a new regime following the liquidity distortions of 2020 to 2022. Central bank intervention suppressed price discovery, forced yield compression, and incentivized duration risk. These features did not dissipate but instead mutated into structural inefficiencies once base rates normalized.

This regime shift is not merely a change in market conditions but a fundamental reordering of risk allocation. The distortions introduced during the zero-rate era have left lasting imprints on capital structures, investor behavior, and regulatory frameworks. The current environment is characterized by four macro forces that define allocator decision frameworks:

1. Regulatory reduction in bank risk appetite
Basel III and its subsequent iterations have fundamentally altered the economics of bank lending. The introduction of output floors, higher risk weights for operationally intensive sectors, and stricter liquidity coverage ratios have elevated the cost of capital for traditional lenders. This is not a temporary adjustment but a permanent shift in the cost structure of regulated financial intermediation.

The impact is most visible in sectors where operational complexity intersects with regulatory scrutiny. Energy infrastructure, for example, faces risk weights of 100% to 150% under Basel III, compared to 50% to 75% pre-crisis. This has led to a 30% to 40% reduction in bank lending to midstream assets in North America since 2019. Similar dynamics are observable in European industrial lending, where risk weights for specialized manufacturing have increased by 25% to 35% over the same period.

Capital that traditionally came from regulated lenders no longer flows at institutional scale or institutional velocity. This has created a structural void in the middle market, particularly for borrowers with EBITDA between €20 million and €100 million. These companies are too large for regional banks but too small for public debt markets, leaving them dependent on private credit providers with the capacity for complex underwriting.

2. Asset level volatility in non-cyclical sectors
The second defining feature of the current regime is the mispricing of real assets with predictable utilization profiles. This is not a function of fundamental weakness but of generalist bias in capital allocation. Heavy oil in Alberta, midstream corridor assets in the Permian Basin, and European industrial SMEs face lower capital availability despite operational resilience.

The mispricing stems from a disconnect between asset-level cash flows and macroeconomic narratives. Heavy oil producers in Alberta, for example, generate stable cash flows with 85% to 90% utilization rates, yet face borrowing costs 200 to 300 basis points higher than comparable assets in the Permian. This is not a reflection of geological risk but of capital market segmentation. Similarly, European industrial SMEs with 20-year customer contracts face spreads 150 to 200 basis points wider than their North American counterparts, despite lower operational volatility.

This mispricing creates opportunities for allocators with the technical capacity to underwrite asset-level durability. The key is not to chase yield but to identify assets where cash flow stability is decoupled from macroeconomic sentiment. This requires a granular understanding of sector-specific drivers - well decline curves in energy, contract renewal rates in logistics, and customer concentration in manufacturing.

3. Institutional drift
The third force shaping the current regime is the drift of allocators toward passive or quasi-passive models during the low-rate era. This drift has left many institutional investors ill-equipped for markets where underwriting precision, operational visibility, and microstructural literacy carry outsized relevance.

The passive drift took multiple forms. Some allocators outsourced credit selection to third-party managers with broad mandates, sacrificing precision for scale. Others adopted index-like exposure to private credit, treating the asset class as a yield enhancement rather than a capital structuring discipline. A third group migrated toward standardized deal structures, prioritizing execution speed over structural optimization.

These approaches were viable in a low-rate environment where capital was abundant and spreads were compressed. They are poorly suited for the current regime, where the marginal dollar of capital is allocated based on underwriting rigor rather than origination velocity. The result is a bifurcation in the market, with a small group of allocators capturing the majority of value while the rest compete for residual opportunities.

4. Fragmentation of middle market deal flow
The final defining feature of the current regime is the fragmentation of middle market deal flow. Many family-controlled or founder-led businesses are engaging in refinancing or recapitalization events under compressed timelines. This creates a window where balance sheet optimization becomes as important as cash flow coverage.

The fragmentation is driven by three factors. First, the maturity wall of 2025 to 2027, where €200 billion to €300 billion of European middle market debt and $300 billion to $400 billion of North American debt will come due. Second, the generational transition in family-owned businesses, where aging founders are seeking liquidity or succession planning. Third, the rise of operational complexity in middle market companies, where supply chain reconfiguration, energy transition, and digital transformation are creating new capital needs.

This fragmentation has two implications for allocators. First, it creates a premium for speed and flexibility in capital deployment. Second, it elevates the importance of structural solutions over standardized deal terms. The most effective allocators in this environment are those that can tailor capital structures to the specific needs of borrowers, rather than forcing borrowers into predefined templates.

This regime shift did not break private credit. It exposed a lack of stewardship discipline among both borrowers and lenders. The market is not suffering from a shortage of capital but from a shortage of capital with the right structural characteristics. The challenge for allocators is not to find yield but to deploy capital in a way that aligns with the new realities of the market.

## Stewardship is not an ethical overlay

It is a capital structuring discipline. It governs continuity. It prevents waste. It aligns time horizons across stakeholders. Stewardship in private credit is not about benevolence or corporate social responsibility. It is about the rigorous application of capital to assets in a way that preserves value, enhances resilience, and ensures continuity.

The concept of stewardship has deep roots in Nordic industrial traditions, particularly the Gnosjö spirit of quiet discipline and covenantal capitalism. In this tradition, capital is not an abstract financial instrument but a tool for building enduring enterprises. The focus is on the long-term alignment of interests between borrowers, lenders, and the broader ecosystem of employees, customers, and communities.

This tradition stands in contrast to the transactional approach that has dominated private credit in recent years. In a transactional model, the goal is to maximize short-term returns through leverage, fees, and exit multiples. In a stewardship model, the goal is to create durable capital structures that can withstand volatility and generate consistent returns over time.

The difference between these two approaches is not philosophical but practical. Stewardship produces better outcomes because it aligns the incentives of all stakeholders. Borrowers benefit from stable capital structures that support long-term growth. Lenders benefit from lower default rates and higher recovery values. Employees and communities benefit from the continuity of well-managed enterprises.

## Technical mechanics of stewardship in private credit

Stewardship in a volatile private credit environment is best understood as a governance system applied to capital. It is a discipline that focuses on the triad of asset durability, balance sheet optimization, and operational accountability. Each component interacts with the others and produces a compounding effect when executed with precision.

### Asset durability

Durable assets provide a stable foundation for private credit. They are characterized by three attributes: predictable cash flows, low correlation with macroeconomic cycles, and high barriers to entry. These attributes are not inherent to any particular sector but are the result of structural positioning within a value chain.

Consider the example of a European industrial equipment leasing company. The durability of this asset is not a function of the equipment itself but of its role in the broader industrial ecosystem. The company leases specialized machinery to manufacturers with long-term contracts, providing a stable stream of cash flows. The equipment is critical to the production process, creating high switching costs for customers. The leasing model aligns the interests of the lessor and lessee, as both parties benefit from the efficient use of the asset.

This type of asset is not immune to economic downturns, but it is resilient. Cash flows may decline by 10% to 15% in a recession, but they do not collapse. The high barriers to entry - regulatory approvals, customer relationships, and operational expertise - protect the asset from competitive erosion. This resilience is the foundation of stewardship in private credit.

The key to identifying durable assets is to focus on the microstructural drivers of cash flow stability. In energy infrastructure, this means understanding well decline curves and contract renewal rates. In logistics, it means analyzing route density and customer concentration. In manufacturing, it means evaluating supply chain integration and customer stickiness.

### Balance sheet optimization

The second pillar of stewardship is balance sheet optimization. This is not about maximizing leverage but about creating capital structures that align with the underlying cash flow profile of the asset. The goal is to minimize the risk of financial distress while preserving the flexibility to invest in growth.

Balance sheet optimization begins with a granular analysis of cash flow volatility. This analysis informs the appropriate mix of debt instruments - senior secured, second lien, unitranche, or mezzanine. It also determines the optimal maturity profile, with a focus on matching the duration of liabilities to the duration of assets.

Consider the example of a North American midstream energy company. The company operates a network of pipelines with long-term take-or-pay contracts, providing a stable stream of cash flows. The optimal capital structure for this asset is not a single tranche of senior debt but a layered structure with multiple maturities and covenants.

The senior tranche might have a 5-year maturity with tight covenants, reflecting the near-term visibility of cash flows. The second lien tranche might have a 7-year maturity with looser covenants, reflecting the longer-term stability of the asset. The mezzanine tranche might have a 10-year maturity with equity-like features, reflecting the upside potential of the business.

This layered structure achieves two objectives. First, it minimizes the risk of financial distress by ensuring that the company has access to capital at multiple points in the cycle. Second, it preserves the flexibility to invest in growth opportunities, such as pipeline expansions or acquisitions.

The key to balance sheet optimization is to avoid the temptation of over-leveraging. In a low-rate environment, it is easy to justify high leverage ratios based on the stability of cash flows. In a volatile environment, this approach is dangerous. The goal is not to maximize returns in the short term but to create a capital structure that can withstand shocks and generate consistent returns over time.

### Operational accountability

The third pillar of stewardship is operational accountability. This is the discipline of ensuring that borrowers have the management capacity, governance structures, and incentive systems to execute on their business plans. Operational accountability is not about micromanaging borrowers but about aligning incentives and creating transparency.

Operational accountability begins with the selection of borrowers. The focus is on companies with strong management teams, clear strategic plans, and robust governance structures. These attributes are not always visible in financial statements but are critical to the long-term success of the investment.

Consider the example of a European industrial SME. The company has a 20-year track record of stable cash flows, a diversified customer base, and a strong management team. The key to operational accountability in this case is to ensure that the management team has the right incentives to execute on its strategic plan.

This might involve structuring the debt with performance-based covenants, such as minimum EBITDA thresholds or maximum capital expenditure limits. It might also involve creating a board of directors with independent members who can provide oversight and guidance. The goal is not to constrain the borrower but to create a framework for disciplined decision-making.

Operational accountability also requires transparency. Borrowers must provide regular reporting on key performance indicators, such as cash flow coverage, customer retention rates, and operational efficiency metrics. This reporting is not a bureaucratic exercise but a tool for identifying risks and opportunities early.

The key to operational accountability is to strike the right balance between oversight and autonomy. Borrowers need the flexibility to respond to changing market conditions, but they also need the discipline to stay focused on their long-term strategic objectives. The role of the lender is not to dictate decisions but to create a framework for disciplined execution.

## Liquidity engineering frameworks

Stewardship in private credit is not just about selecting the right assets and structuring the right capital. It is also about engineering liquidity to ensure that capital can be deployed and redeployed efficiently. Liquidity engineering is the discipline of creating structures that balance the illiquidity of private assets with the liquidity needs of investors.

The challenge of liquidity engineering is to reconcile two competing objectives. On the one hand, private credit assets are inherently illiquid, with long investment horizons and limited secondary markets. On the other hand, investors need the flexibility to rebalance portfolios, meet redemption requests, and respond to changing market conditions.

The solution is to create layered liquidity structures that match the cash flow profile of the assets with the liquidity needs of the investors. This involves three key components: capital call structures, distribution waterfalls, and secondary market mechanisms.

### Capital call structures

Capital call structures are the foundation of liquidity engineering in private credit. They allow investors to commit capital upfront but deploy it only as needed, preserving liquidity until the capital is actually invested. The key is to design capital call structures that align with the investment pace of the fund.

Consider the example of a private credit fund with a 5-year investment period. The fund might structure its capital calls in three tranches: an initial tranche of 30% at closing, a second tranche of 40% in year 2, and a final tranche of 30% in year 4. This structure ensures that investors retain liquidity until the capital is needed, while also providing the fund with the flexibility to deploy capital as opportunities arise.

The design of capital call structures must balance two competing objectives. On the one hand, frequent capital calls can create administrative burdens for investors and increase the risk of default. On the other hand, infrequent capital calls can lead to idle cash and reduce the efficiency of capital deployment. The optimal structure is one that minimizes administrative burdens while maximizing the efficiency of capital deployment.

### Distribution waterfalls

Distribution waterfalls are the second component of liquidity engineering. They determine the order in which cash flows are distributed to investors, creating a predictable pattern of liquidity. The key is to design distribution waterfalls that align with the cash flow profile of the assets and the liquidity needs of the investors.

Consider the example of a private credit fund with a 10-year term. The fund might structure its distribution waterfall in three tiers: a return of capital tier, a preferred return tier, and a carried interest tier. The return of capital tier ensures that investors receive their principal back before any profits are distributed. The preferred return tier ensures that investors receive a minimum return on their capital before the fund manager receives any carried interest. The carried interest tier ensures that the fund manager is incentivized to generate strong returns for investors.

The design of distribution waterfalls must balance two competing objectives. On the one hand, a simple waterfall can create transparency and predictability for investors. On the other hand, a complex waterfall can create misalignments between the interests of the fund manager and the investors. The optimal structure is one that creates transparency and predictability while aligning the interests of all stakeholders.

### Secondary market mechanisms

Secondary market mechanisms are the third component of liquidity engineering. They provide investors with the flexibility to exit their investments before the end of the fund term, creating liquidity in an otherwise illiquid asset class. The key is to design secondary market mechanisms that balance the liquidity needs of investors with the stability needs of the fund.

Consider the example of a private credit fund with a 10-year term. The fund might create a secondary market mechanism that allows investors to sell their interests to other investors after a 3-year lock-up period. This mechanism provides investors with the flexibility to exit their investments early, while also ensuring that the fund has a stable capital base for the first 3 years.

The design of secondary market mechanisms must balance two competing objectives. On the one hand, a liquid secondary market can create price volatility and destabilize the fund. On the other hand, an illiquid secondary market can create a mismatch between the liquidity needs of investors and the illiquidity of the assets. The optimal structure is one that provides liquidity without destabilizing the fund.

## Allocator calibration models

Stewardship in private credit requires more than just the right assets, the right capital structures, and the right liquidity engineering. It also requires the right allocator calibration models. These models are the tools that investors use to measure risk, allocate capital, and monitor performance.

The challenge of allocator calibration is to create models that are both precise and flexible. On the one hand, models must be precise enough to capture the nuances of private credit risk. On the other hand, they must be flexible enough to adapt to changing market conditions.

The solution is to create layered calibration models that combine quantitative analysis with qualitative judgment. This involves three key components: risk measurement frameworks, capital allocation models, and performance monitoring systems.
